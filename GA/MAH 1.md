Donald Maclean: Research Answers

1. What were his origins?

Maclean was born in London on 25 May 1913. He was the son of Liberal cabinet minister Sir Donald Maclean, attended Gresham’s School and studied modern languages at Cambridge, graduating with first-class honours in 1934.
Highlighted source: “was born in London”

2. Why did he support communism?

After his father’s death in 1932, Maclean moved sharply to the political left and joined communist organisations at Cambridge. He blamed capitalism for unemployment, low wages, class prejudice and other social problems.
Highlighted source: “The economic situation”

3. How was he recruited?

Kim Philby identified Maclean as a possible recruit and introduced him to Soviet intelligence officer Arnold Deutsch. Deutsch considered him serious and well connected, gave him the code name ORPHAN and ordered him to distance himself from known communists.
Highlighted source: “codename Orphan”

4. How was he connected to the other Cambridge spies?

Maclean knew Philby, Guy Burgess, Anthony Blunt and John Cairncross through Cambridge’s communist circles. He suggested Burgess as a recruit; later Philby warned Moscow that Maclean was in danger, Blunt contacted Soviet controller Yuri Modin, and Burgess helped Maclean escape.
Highlighted source: “suggested to Deutsch”

5. What jobs did he hold?

Maclean joined the Foreign Office and became third secretary at the British Embassy in Paris in 1938. He later served in Washington, London and Egypt, worked on the Combined Policy Committee and became head of the Foreign Office’s American section.
Highlighted source: “appointed third secretary”

6. How did he obtain information?

Maclean used his legitimate diplomatic access to obtain confidential telegrams and documents, including material about nuclear planning. The supplied websites do not clearly explain whether he personally copied or photographed the documents.
Highlighted source: “passed vital documents”

7. How did he pass information to the Soviets?

Maclean dealt with Soviet handlers including Arnold Deutsch, Theodore Maly and later Yuri Modin. VENONA showed that Maclean, code-named HOMER, regularly contacted his Soviet controller in New York while using visits to his pregnant wife as cover.
Highlighted source: “Soviet control”

8. What important secrets did he pass?

Maclean passed documents about nuclear planning and obtained access to American atomic-energy information through the Combined Policy Committee. VENONA also revealed that Moscow received the complete text of a private 1945 telegram from Churchill to Truman.
Highlighted source: “nuclear planning”
Highlighted source: Churchill–Truman telegram

9. What damage did his spying cause?

His spying gave the Soviet Union information about Western nuclear policy and private communications between Allied leaders. The sources do not prove that he directly exposed agents, but Philby feared that Maclean’s interrogation would compromise the entire British spy network.
Highlighted source: “entire British spy network”

10. When was he first suspected?

In 1939–40, Soviet defector Walter Krivitsky described a Soviet agent in the Foreign Office whose background partly resembled Maclean, but MI5 did not follow the clue. In April 1951, VENONA information about HOMER’s meetings in New York allowed investigators to identify Maclean.
Highlighted source: “vital clue”

11. What happened when he was exposed?

Maclean was not confronted, prosecuted or offered immunity because Philby warned Moscow and Modin arranged his escape. On 25 May 1951, Burgess collected him in a rented car, and they travelled through France and Switzerland before reaching Moscow.
Highlighted source: “rented car”

12. What happened afterward?

Maclean became a Soviet citizen and worked for the Soviet Foreign Ministry and the Institute of World Economic and International Relations. He published British Policy Since Suez in 1970 and died in Moscow in 1983; the supplied evidence records no clear expression of regret.
Highlighted source: “became a Soviet citizen”
